Getting there & getting around

Flights, ferries, border crossings, car hire, buses, money and mobile data — the logistics behind a smooth beach trip to Montenegro and Albania.

By Daniel · Updated 24 October 2026 · About 15 minutes to read

Photo: Llogara Pass by Albinfo, CC BY-SA 4.0

Montenegro and Albania are close to the rest of Europe — most of the continent is within a three-hour flight — but getting there and moving around takes a little more planning than in Croatia or Greece. There are fewer flights, the two countries aren't in the EU or the Schengen Area, and public transport works differently from what most visitors are used to. This guide covers everything you need to plan the logistics, from choosing an airport to crossing the border and getting mobile data.

Always double-check: flight routes, ferry timetables and entry rules change from season to season. Use this guide to plan, then confirm the details with the airline, the ferry operator and official government websites before you book.

Which airport should you fly into?

AirportBest forTime to the beach
Tivat (TIV), MontenegroKotor, Budva, Sveti Stefan15–40 min
Podgorica (TGD), MontenegroBudva, Petrovac, Bar, Ulcinj45–90 min
Dubrovnik (DBV), CroatiaHerceg Novi and the Bay of Kotor1–2 h, depending on the border queue
Tirana (TIA), AlbaniaDurrës, Vlorë, the Riviera45 min to Durrës, 3–4 h to Himarë
Corfu (CFU), GreeceSarandë and Ksamil, by ferryFerry of about 30–70 min

Our pick: for a Montenegro-only trip, fly into Tivat. For the Albanian Riviera, the Corfu + ferry combination is often the fastest way to reach Ksamil, and it avoids the long drive from Tirana. For a trip covering both countries, fly into Tivat and out of Tirana or Corfu.

Tips for finding cheaper flights

  • Compare all five airports. Tivat is small and its summer flights fill up fast; Podgorica and Dubrovnik are sometimes much cheaper, even after adding the transfer.
  • Consider open-jaw tickets: flying into one airport and out of another (for example Tivat in, Corfu out) saves you backtracking.
  • Look outside July and August. Fares and car hire prices can drop sharply in late May, June and September.
  • See our budget travel tips for more ways to save.

Airport transfers

  • Tivat: taxis to Kotor take about 15–20 minutes and to Budva about 30 minutes. Agree the fare before you get in, or book a transfer through your accommodation.
  • Podgorica: the airport is just outside the city. Taxis and transfers to the coast take about an hour; public transport from the airport is limited.
  • Dubrovnik: transfers to the Bay of Kotor cross the Croatia–Montenegro border, so allow extra time in summer.
  • Tirana: a regular airport bus connects with central Tirana, and taxis are easy to find. Most car rental companies have desks at the airport.

Ferries

  • Corfu – Sarandë: several crossings a day in summer, with fast boats taking roughly 30–40 minutes and slower ferries over an hour. Bring your passport or ID card, because this is an external EU border. Book ahead for July and August.
  • Italy – Montenegro: overnight ferries run between Bari and Bar.
  • Italy – Albania: regular ferries connect Bari, Brindisi and Ancona with Durrës, and in summer there are also crossings to Vlorë.

Bringing your own car on the ferry is a good option for longer stays: you avoid cross-border rental fees and can pack more. Check that your insurance covers both countries, and book a cabin for overnight crossings in summer.

Crossing the border

The two main road crossings between Montenegro and Albania are:

  • Sukobin – Muriqan: on the coastal route between Ulcinj and Shkodër. It is the most convenient crossing for a beach trip.
  • Božaj – Hani i Hotit: on the route between Podgorica and Shkodër, along Lake Skadar.

Both are usually quick outside peak season. On summer weekends, queues of an hour or more are common, so cross early in the morning. Neither country is in the Schengen Area, so your passport or ID will be checked on each side.

What to have ready at the border

  • Passport or national ID card for everyone in the car
  • Driving licence and the car's registration document
  • Proof of insurance valid in both countries (a Green Card or equivalent)
  • The rental company's written cross-border authorisation, if it's a hire car
Entry documents: EU citizens can generally enter both countries with a national ID card, while most other visitors need a passport. Many nationalities, including the UK, US, Canada and Australia, can usually stay visa-free for short visits. Always confirm the current rules for your nationality on official government websites.

Driving and car hire

Renting a car

  • Cross-border permission: tell your rental company that you will cross the border. You will need written authorisation and valid insurance for both countries, and usually a cross-border fee.
  • One-way rentals between countries are often very expensive. Renting one car in each country is frequently cheaper.
  • Deposit: most companies block a deposit on a credit card. Check the amount and whether debit cards are accepted.
  • Insurance: consider full cover or a zero-excess option — narrow roads, tight parking and gravel tracks mean small scratches are common.
  • Inspect the car: photograph or film the car from all sides at pick-up, including the tyres and windscreen.

On the road

  • Montenegro: dipped headlights are required at all times, and the coastal road gets very congested between Budva and Bečići in summer.
  • Albania: the main roads are much better than their reputation, but the coastal road (SH8) has many tight bends. Watch for animals, slow vehicles and drivers overtaking on blind corners, and avoid driving on mountain roads after dark.
  • Police checks are common in both countries and usually routine. Keep your documents within reach, and drive within the speed limits — speed cameras and radar checks are frequent.
  • Parking: it is limited and paid in Kotor, Budva and Ksamil. Choose accommodation with parking included.
  • Fuel: petrol stations are plentiful on main roads, but fill up before mountain routes.

Buses and public transport

Montenegro has frequent coastal buses linking Herceg Novi, Kotor, Tivat, Budva, Petrovac, Bar and Ulcinj. They are cheap and reliable, and tickets are bought at bus stations or on board. Some bus stations add a small platform fee.

Albania relies on a mix of buses and furgons (shared minibuses). Departures from Tirana to Vlorë and Sarandë are frequent. Along the Riviera, buses run in summer between Vlorë, Dhërmi, Himarë and Sarandë, but timetables are rarely posted online, so ask your host or at the local bus stop.

Between the countries, buses connect Ulcinj and Podgorica with Shkodër and Tirana, especially in summer.

Taxis and boat taxis

Taxis are easy to find in towns. Where meters aren't used, agree the fare before you set off, or ask your accommodation to call one. On the coast, boat taxis are a fun and practical way to reach beaches that are hard to get to by road — from Budva to Sveti Nikola island, or from Himarë and Ksamil to nearby coves.

Money

  • Montenegro uses the euro, even though it is not in the EU. Cards are widely accepted in towns, but small beach bars may only take cash.
  • Albania uses the lek (ALL). Euros are often accepted on the coast, but usually at a worse rate. ATMs are easy to find in towns, but scarcer near the remote coves.
  • When a card machine or ATM offers to charge you in your home currency, decline and pay in the local currency.
  • Check your bank's foreign transaction and ATM fees before you go; a fee-free travel card can save a lot over two weeks.

Mobile data

Neither country is in the EU, so EU "roam like at home" rules do not automatically apply. Check your plan before you go. Otherwise, a local prepaid SIM or a travel eSIM is usually cheap and easy to set up. Download offline maps as well, because signal drops on mountain roads such as the Llogara Pass.

Health, insurance and emergencies

  • Travel insurance: the European Health Insurance Card (EHIC) is not generally valid in either country, so take out travel insurance that covers medical care and, if you plan to hike or raft, outdoor activities.
  • Pharmacies (apoteka in Montenegro, farmaci in Albania) are easy to find in towns and can help with minor problems.
  • Emergency numbers in Montenegro: 112 (general), 122 (police), 124 (ambulance), 123 (fire).
  • Emergency numbers in Albania: 129 (police), 127 (ambulance), 128 (fire).

Accommodation registration and tourist tax

Both countries expect foreign visitors to be registered at their accommodation, and a small tourist tax is usually charged per person per night. Hotels and most licensed apartments handle this for you. If you stay in a private room, ask your host whether they have registered you, and keep any registration slip you're given until you leave the country.

Getting there FAQ

Is there a train between Montenegro and Albania?

No — there is no passenger train between the two countries. Use a bus, a transfer or a car. Within Montenegro, the Bar–Belgrade railway is a scenic option for reaching the mountains.

Can I take a rental car from Montenegro to Albania?

Usually yes, with the rental company's written permission, insurance valid in both countries and a cross-border fee. Dropping the car in another country is often expensive or not allowed.

What's the cheapest way to reach the Albanian Riviera?

Often a budget flight to Tirana followed by a bus to Vlorë, Himarë or Sarandë. If flights to Corfu are cheap, the ferry to Sarandë can be faster overall.
