Albania

Albania travel tips: 21 things to know before you go

Albania is one of Europe's most rewarding and least understood countries. These are the practical details and cultural quirks that make a first trip smoother.

By Daniel · Updated 24 October 2026 · About 18 minutes to read

Photo: Berat by Avi1111 dr. avishai teicher, CC BY-SA 4.0

For decades Albania was one of the most isolated countries on Earth. Under the communist regime of Enver Hoxha, which ruled from the end of the Second World War until his death in 1985, ordinary Albanians could not travel abroad and very few foreigners were allowed in. The country only opened up in the early 1990s, and for years afterwards it was known more for its problems than for its beauty.

That has changed completely. Tourism has boomed over the last decade, and Albania now draws visitors with Mediterranean beaches, Alpine-style mountains, Ottoman towns, Greek and Roman ruins and some of the friendliest people in Europe — all at prices well below most of the continent. It also has a few habits and quirks that catch first-time visitors off guard. This guide collects the practical details that make a first trip smoother, from money and manners to food, transport and the places most people miss.

Albania at a glance: capital Tirana · population around 2.4 million · currency lek (ALL) · language Albanian · time zone CET (same as Spain, Italy and Germany) · plugs type C/F, 230 V · drives on the right.

When to visit Albania

Albania has a Mediterranean climate on the coast and a cooler, more continental climate in the mountains, so the best time to go depends on what you want to do.

SeasonWhat it's likeBest for
April–MayWarm days, green landscapes, wildflowers; the sea is still cool. Few tourists.Cities, Berat, Gjirokastër, Butrint, hiking at low altitude
JuneHot and sunny, sea warming up, prices still reasonable.Beaches without the crowds; the mountain season starts
July–AugustVery hot (often above 35 °C inland), beaches packed, highest prices.Beach life, nightlife, the Albanian Alps
SeptemberWarm sea, calmer beaches, lower prices.The best all-round month
OctoberMild and often sunny, many beach businesses closing.Cities, food, hiking, road trips
November–MarchCool and rainy on the coast, snow in the mountains. Many coastal hotels closed.Tirana and city breaks on a tight budget

If you can only choose one month, choose September: the sea is at its warmest, the summer crowds have gone home and prices fall noticeably after the first week.

Albania's regions in brief

Albania is small — about the size of Belgium — but its regions feel very different from each other. Here is a quick overview to help you decide where to go.

  • Tirana and the centre: a colourful, fast-changing capital with excellent cafés, museums about the communist past and a cable car up Mount Dajti. Most visitors spend one or two nights here.
  • The north and the Albanian Alps: wild valleys, stone villages and the famous Theth–Valbona hike. Shkodër, a relaxed lakeside city, is the gateway.
  • Central Albania: Berat and Durrës. Durrës has a Roman amphitheatre and long sandy beaches that are popular with Albanian families.
  • The Albanian Riviera: the coast from Vlorë to Sarandë, where you'll find the clearest water, the white-pebble beaches and the coves like Gjipe and Jale.
  • The south and interior: Gjirokastër, the Blue Eye spring, the ancient city of Butrint and quiet mountain villages.

Money and costs

1. Use the lek, and carry cash

The currency is the lek (ALL); the plural you'll see on signs is lekë. Euros are widely accepted on the coast and in Tirana, but the rate used is usually rounded in the seller's favour, so pay in lek when you can.

Cards work in hotels, supermarkets, petrol stations and many restaurants in cities. However, small cafés, bakeries, beach bars, furgons and family guesthouses often take cash only. ATMs are easy to find in towns, but scarce in mountain villages and near remote beaches, so withdraw before you head off. Check your bank's fees: some Albanian ATMs charge a fixed fee per withdrawal, so taking out larger amounts less often is cheaper.

2. Watch out for "old lek"

Albania revalued its currency in 1965, removing a zero, but some people — especially older Albanians and market sellers — still quote prices in "old lek", which are ten times higher. If a coffee is suddenly "1,000", it's almost certainly 100 lek. It's not a scam, just an old habit. When in doubt, look at the price on the menu or ask "lekë të reja?" (new lek?).

3. It's still good value — but not "dirt cheap" everywhere

Albania is generally cheaper than Montenegro, Croatia or Greece, especially for food, coffee and local transport. A filling lunch of byrek and a drink, or a plate of grilled meat with salad in a local restaurant, still costs a few euros away from the tourist areas.

Prices in Ksamil, Sarandë and parts of the Riviera in July and August have risen sharply in recent years, though, and seafront restaurants and sunbeds there can cost almost as much as in Montenegro. To stretch your budget, travel in June or September, eat one street back from the seafront and stay in family-run guesthouses. Culture and etiquette

4. Head gestures can be reversed

Traditionally, Albanians nod their head up and down to mean no and shake it side to side to mean yes — the opposite of most of Europe. Younger Albanians and people working in tourism often use Western gestures, which makes it even more confusing. Listen for the words: po means yes and jo means no.

5. Hospitality is taken seriously

Albanian hospitality is legendary, rooted in an old code of honour called the besa — a promise of protection and trust. During the Second World War, the besa led many Albanian families to shelter Jewish refugees, and Albania ended the war with more Jews than it had at the start.

Today you'll see it in smaller ways. Don't be surprised if a guesthouse owner brings you fruit from the garden, a Turkish-style coffee or a glass of homemade raki. Accepting graciously — even just a sip — is part of the experience, and refusing everything can come across as cold.

6. Join the evening xhiro

In almost every town, people come out in the early evening for the xhiro — a slow stroll along the main street or seafront. Families, couples and groups of friends walk up and down, stop for an ice cream and chat with neighbours. It's the best moment to people-watch and feel the rhythm of local life, and it's free.

7. A country of religious coexistence

Albania has Muslim, Orthodox and Catholic communities, and mosques and churches often stand side by side. In 1967 the communist government declared the country the world's first officially atheist state, and religious practice was banned until 1990. Today, Albanians are proud of their tradition of tolerance, and mixed families are common.

When visiting religious sites, dress modestly — covered shoulders and knees — and take your shoes off before entering a mosque. Avoid visiting during prayer times unless invited.

8. A few words go a long way

Albanian is a unique branch of the Indo-European language family, unrelated to its neighbours' languages. Young people often speak good English, and many older Albanians understand Italian or Greek. Learning a few words will earn you big smiles:

Food and drink

9. Eat the classics

Albanian food is hearty, seasonal and built on excellent olive oil, fresh vegetables, dairy and grilled meat, with influences from Italy, Greece and Turkey. Much of what you eat will have been grown nearby, and portions are generous. Look for:

  • Byrek — flaky filo pie filled with cheese, spinach, onion and tomato, or minced meat. Bought warm from a bakery in the morning, it's the ultimate cheap breakfast.
  • Tavë kosi — baked lamb with rice under a tangy topping of yoghurt and eggs, often called the national dish.
  • Fërgesë — peppers, tomatoes and cottage cheese, baked in a clay pot and eaten with bread. A Tirana speciality.
  • Qofte — small grilled meatballs or sausages, often served with bread, salad and yoghurt.
  • Speca të mbushura — peppers stuffed with rice, meat or cheese.
  • Fresh fish and seafood on the coast, usually grilled simply with lemon, olive oil and herbs. Fish is often sold by weight, so ask the price first.
  • Trilece — a very sweet, milky sponge cake that has become a favourite dessert.

Vegetarians will do fine: salads, grilled vegetables, byrek, fërgesë, beans (fasule) and fresh cheese are everywhere. Vegans will need to ask more questions, as dairy appears in many dishes.

10. Coffee is a social ritual

Albania has one of the highest numbers of cafés per person in the world. Espresso is cheap and excellent, Turkish-style coffee (kafe turke) is still common, and sitting over one coffee for an hour is completely normal. Coffee is where business gets done, friends catch up and visitors are welcomed.

11. Try the raki — carefully

Raki is a strong fruit brandy, usually made from grapes and often homemade. It's offered as a sign of welcome, drunk before meals and toasted with "Gëzuar!". Homemade raki can be very strong, so sip slowly. Albania also produces good local wines and beers that are worth trying.

12. Drink bottled water

Tap water quality varies around the country. Most visitors stick to bottled or filtered water, which is cheap and sold everywhere. Bringing a filter bottle cuts down on plastic and saves money over a long trip.

13. Tipping

Tipping isn't mandatory, but rounding up the bill or leaving around 10% in restaurants is appreciated, especially in tourist areas. For taxis and cafés, rounding up is enough. Guides and drivers on day tours usually appreciate a tip if you enjoyed the trip.

Getting around

14. Furgons: the local way to travel

Albania's passenger railway network is very limited, so the country runs on buses and furgons — shared minibuses that leave when they're full. They're cheap and go almost everywhere, but timetables are rarely online and departure points can change.

  • Ask your host or at the bus station the day before you travel.
  • Travel in the morning, when departures are most frequent; afternoon services can be rare in smaller towns.
  • Pay the driver or conductor in cash, usually at the end of the journey.
  • In Tirana, buses to different regions leave from different terminals, so check which one you need.

15. Driving: doable, with care

Main roads have improved dramatically, and a rental car is the best way to explore the Riviera and the south at your own pace. Driving styles are more improvised than in Western Europe, though. Expect tight mountain bends, slow trucks, animals on the road and drivers overtaking on blind corners.

  • Avoid driving mountain roads at night, when hazards are hard to see.
  • Keep your licence, passport and rental papers handy for police checks, which are common and usually routine.
  • Fill up when you can on mountain routes, and carry some cash for fuel in rural areas.
  • Many roads to beaches and villages are steep and narrow; a small car is easier to handle and park.

16. Is Albania safe?

Albania is generally considered a safe destination for travellers, and violent crime against tourists is rare. The main risks are the same as anywhere popular: pickpocketing in crowded places, overcharging in tourist areas and, above all, road traffic. Use common sense with valuables on busy beaches, agree taxi fares in advance and take extra care on mountain roads. As always, check your government's official travel advice before you go.

Places most visitors miss

17. Berat, the "city of a thousand windows"

Berat's white Ottoman houses climb the hillside in rows, their many windows facing the Osum river — hence the nickname. Cross the old stone bridge to the Gorica quarter for the classic view, then climb to the castle at the top, which is still inhabited and full of small Byzantine churches. Stay overnight to see the town lit up in the evening, when day-trippers have gone. Berat and Gjirokastër are listed together as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

18. Gjirokastër, the "city of stone"

About an hour inland from Sarandë, Gjirokastër is built in grey stone, from its fortified tower-houses to its roofs. Visit the huge hilltop castle, wander the old bazaar and step inside one of the restored Ottoman houses to see how wealthy families once lived. It makes a perfect day trip — or a cooler overnight escape — from the beach.

19. Butrint and the Blue Eye

Near Ksamil, Butrint National Park protects an ancient city inhabited from Greek times through the Roman, Byzantine and Venetian eras. Its theatre, baptistery and walls sit in a lagoon landscape full of birdlife. Go early in the morning to beat the heat. Inland from Sarandë, the Blue Eye (Syri i Kaltër) is a spring where intensely blue water wells up from a deep pool. It's very popular, so visit early or late in the day.

20. Theth and the Albanian Alps

In the far north, the Albanian Alps (Bjeshkët e Nemuna, often translated as the "Accursed Mountains") offer some of the most dramatic hiking in Europe. A classic route connects the villages of Theth and Valbona over a high mountain pass — a full day's hike — and can be combined with a ferry trip across the fjord-like Lake Koman. The hiking season runs roughly from June to September. Stay in family guesthouses in the villages, where dinner is usually homemade.

21. Bunkers and the communist past

Under Enver Hoxha, Albania built more than 170,000 concrete bunkers to defend against an invasion that never came. You'll spot them on beaches, in fields and on hillsides all over the country; some have been painted, turned into cafés or used as storage.

In Tirana, two former bunkers have been turned into the Bunk'Art museums, which tell the story of the regime and its victims. The House of Leaves museum, in the former secret police headquarters, shows how ordinary people were watched. They're sobering, but essential for understanding modern Albania.

A sample 7-day Albania itinerary

Short on time? This route combines culture, mountains and beaches in a week. It works by car, or by bus if you're happy to travel more slowly.

  • Day 1 — Tirana: Skanderbeg Square, Bunk'Art, the Blloku café district and the Dajti cable car.
  • Day 2 — Berat: about 2 hours south. Explore the old quarters and sleep in the castle.
  • Day 3 — Gjirokastër: drive or bus south, stopping for lunch on the way. Visit the castle and bazaar.
  • Day 4 — Blue Eye and Ksamil: stop at the Blue Eye in the morning, then swim at Ksamil.
  • Day 5 — Butrint and Sarandë: early visit to Butrint, afternoon on the beach, sunset on the Sarandë promenade.
  • Day 6 — The Riviera: drive north along the coast, with swims at Borsh and Porto Palermo, and sleep in Himarë.
  • Day 7 — Gjipe and the Llogara Pass: hike down to Gjipe, then cross the Llogara Pass back towards Vlorë and Tirana.

Quick practical checklist

  • Plugs are type C/F (European two-pin), 230 V
  • Albania isn't in the EU — check your phone's roaming and consider a local SIM or eSIM
  • Many nationalities can visit visa-free for short stays; confirm on official sites before travelling
  • Carry small lek notes for furgons, cafés and beach bars
  • Download offline maps for mountain roads
  • Bring water shoes — most beaches are pebbly
  • Pack modest clothing for visits to mosques, churches and monasteries
  • Get travel insurance that covers hiking if you're heading to the Alps

Albania FAQ

How many days do you need in Albania?

A week is enough to see Tirana, Berat or Gjirokastër and a few beaches on the Riviera. With 10–14 days you can add the Albanian Alps in the north, or combine Albania with Montenegro.

Is English widely spoken in Albania?

Many young Albanians speak good English, and so do most people working in hotels and restaurants in tourist areas. In rural areas, Italian or Greek may be more useful, and a few words of Albanian are always appreciated.

Can I use euros in Albania?

Often, yes — especially on the coast and in Tirana. But the exchange rate applied is usually worse than paying in lek, so it's best to withdraw lek from an ATM and use euros only as a backup.

Do I need a car in Albania?

Not necessarily. Buses and furgons connect all the main towns cheaply. A car makes it much easier to reach remote beaches, mountain villages and viewpoints, and to travel at your own pace.
